Three ways to compute the interface flux

A finite-volume method updates each cell with the fluxes through its two faces. At each face the left and right states form a small Riemann problem, the same kind of problem as the whole shock tube. The schemes differ in how they approximate its solution.

Roe

Linearizes the Riemann problem around the Roe-averaged state and solves it exactly: F = ½(FL + FR) − ½ Σ |λk| αk Kk, with eigenvalues u − a, u, u + a. It resolves all three waves, but it can let through an expansion shock at a sonic point, so this code uses Harten's entropy fix on |u ± a|.

HLLC

Assumes only the fastest left and right waves, SL and SR, plus the contact wave S* in between, with two constant star states. HLL without the "C" smears the contact badly; restoring it gives contact resolution close to Roe. It needs no entropy fix and is robust at strong shocks and expansions.

AUSM+

Splits the flux into a convective part, carried by an interface Mach number m½ built from split polynomials M±(M), and a pressure part p½ = P+(ML) pL + P−(MR) pR. It needs no eigenvectors, so it is cheap and extends easily to other equations.

The three waves

Breaking the diaphragm gives an expansion fan moving left, a contact discontinuity (a jump in density and internal energy, with no jump in pressure or velocity), and a shock moving right. Switch the tab to pressure: the contact disappears.

What to look for

  • The contact is smeared over more cells than the shock. A shock compresses itself, while a contact is a linear wave that only spreads.
  • 2nd order makes all jumps much sharper. Compare the L1 errors.
  • Zoom into the contact and the shock to compare Roe, HLLC and AUSM+ point by point.

The AUSM+ start-up error

At t = 0 the gas is at rest, so m½ = 0 and AUSM+ moves no mass or energy across the diaphragm in the first step, only the pressure force. The cells next to it gain momentum without energy. This leaves a small bump near x = 0.5, and above CFL ≈ 0.65 the first step already makes the pressure negative.
